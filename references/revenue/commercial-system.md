# Offer, pricing, and unit economics

## Offer definition

Define:

- Buyer and user
- Trigger and desired outcome
- Promise and proof requirement
- Included deliverables and explicit exclusions
- Adoption path and customer responsibilities
- Fulfillment constraints and support burden
- Risk handling without invented scarcity or guarantees that cannot be honored
- Upgrade, renewal, or follow-on path

Generate no more than three materially different offer options: simplest to buy, proof-heavy, and expansion-oriented. Explain when each is appropriate.

## Pricing sequence

1. Calculate the fully loaded delivery cost and minimum acceptable contribution.
2. Estimate value and willingness-to-pay from real evidence; label estimates as hypotheses.
3. Choose a value metric the customer understands and that scales with delivered value.
4. Choose flat, project, retainer, usage, outcome, or hybrid pricing.
5. Define packages with clear buyer/outcome boundaries, not arbitrary feature hiding.
6. Set discount and exception rules from economics, not improvisation.
7. Define a test and a review date.

Margin is `(price - variable cost) / price`. Markup is `(price - cost) / cost`. Never substitute one for the other.

## Unit economics

At minimum state:

- Acquisition cost, including founder selling time when material
- Revenue per customer and payment timing
- Variable delivery/support/payment cost
- Contribution margin
- Retention or repeat-purchase assumption
- Payback period
- Capacity consumed per engagement

For an early business without reliable cohorts, use ranges and sensitivity analysis. Do not fabricate CAC, churn, lifetime, or willingness-to-pay from industry averages.

## Commercial artifact

Create a recomputable offer/price card:

| Offer | Buyer/outcome | Includes | Excludes | Price | Variable cost | Contribution margin | Proof needed | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|

The human owner approves the final price, discounts, guarantees, payment terms, and capacity commitments.
